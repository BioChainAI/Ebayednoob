What We Have Made, Measured

Before we build a hyperbolic BERT, we take stock. This repository already gives a language system exact geometry, exact addresses and strong integrity checks. When the survey ran, none of the four repositories gave it a subword tokenizer or a vocabulary (Phase 1 has since built both, in Python), and none gave it learned embeddings, an encoder or gradients (Phase 4 has since trained static embeddings by gradient descent, in Python: Explainer 12). This page sorts what exists from what is missing, with the measurements, and ends with a lab you can type words into.

01

Where this sits

This is the analysis the rest of Hyperbolic Lexicon stands on. Its focus node is Engram Mind’s Eye: the system whose words, codec and bridges this one starts from, and the one it bridges back into.

Phase 0 is analysis, measurement spikes and a preregistration; there is no model code yet. This page rests on a read-only survey of four repositories (Ebayednoob, this site; Owl Academy; BioChain-AI; SHD-CCP) and of the literature, re-checked by an adversarial cross-check whose corrections win. Other numbers come from the design spec (its planning measurements are called “a planning check”), from Phase 0’s two pins (WordNet 3.0 and UD English EWT), or from the existing systems’ own pages. Spikes S1–S7 were still running when it was written; nothing here quotes them (Explainer 8).

How to read the marks

exact: integers, rationals or bytes, identical in every engine. float: floating point, rounded at every step. Decisions use the ledger’s tags: SUPPORTED REFUTED INCONCLUSIVE.

Literature numbers carry the survey’s grade. Verified: matched against the paper’s own page or repository. Likely: from a search summary of a primary source that could not be opened (many paper hosts were blocked here then). Secondary: only from someone else’s summary.

H², H³
The hyperbolic plane and 3-space. Room grows exponentially with distance, as a tree’s nodes multiply with depth.
Hyperboloid
The points with x₀² − x₁² − … − xₙ² = 1, x₀ > 0: the “sheet” of the Lorentz model. The Poincaré disk and ball draw the same space inside a unit circle or sphere.
The norm-5 tree
Words in the integer quaternions 1±2i, 1±2j, 1±2k that never undo the previous letter. An n-letter word multiplies out to one quaternion of norm 5ⁿ; right division reads it back.
GF(2)
Arithmetic on bits, where 1 + 1 = 0: adding is XOR.

The orbital core

orbital_core.js (scripts/geometric-build) holds the orbital calculators’ exact code: BigInt fractions and quaternions, the Cayley map, SHA-256, the 64-bit Einstein tile, Hurwitz Form 1 (flagged lossy), the Lemma 15 chain, the triple-quad formula, the invertible gauge (s, X, Y), the readout and the 720 → 72 → 10 pump. Its Node test passes 18/18 and the four orbital pages rebuild byte-identical (how). For language it offers SHA-256 and byte helpers for the Lexicon’s records. None of it learns.

Two things called “Merkle”

A Merkle root is one hash that commits to a whole list: hash each item, then hash the hashes in pairs, level by level, to a single value. Change any item and the root changes. Biochains use this binary tree. The orbital core’s “Lemma 15 Merkle chain” is a linear backward chain instead, each link hashing its item with the next link’s hash. They are not interchangeable, so the Lexicon names them apart (Lemma 15 note).

Measured costs. A 1,572-packet conversation runs in 1.4 s; 2,000 address round trips take 63 ms. The fence takes 292 ms on 300 points in H³ and grows as N²; the guard takes 13 ms on 37 points and grows as N⁴. Neither reaches a vocabulary of 10⁴–10⁵ points without sampling.

03

engram/1: a checksum, not a token id

engram/1 is where the existing systems turn a word into numbers. So it is worth knowing exactly how little it carries.

How a key is made. Each letter A–Z has a fixed 64-bit grid from a seeded generator (Mulberry32), and a word’s key is the XOR of its letters’ grids. Case is ignored; only A–Z count, so digits, apostrophes and non-ASCII letters are ignored.

What survives. XOR is addition in GF(2), so a letter that appears twice cancels itself. The key depends only on which letters appear an odd number of times: at most 226 ≈ 67 million keys out of 264. The survey found the 26 grids independent over GF(2) (rank 26), so distinct letter-parity patterns always give distinct keys.

No similarity. Distances between distinct words look random: a mean Hamming distance of 32.4 of 64 over 190 pairs (min 20, max 44); cat/cats 30, run/running 31, king/queen 26. Engram’s E9b found recall after a one-letter substitution at chance: 0.0002 against 0.0021, the difference’s interval [−0.0017, 0.0004]. Engram’s 477-token dialogue store has 458 distinct keys.

Why it cannot be a token id

A token id must give every vocabulary entry its own number, and a model learns one embedding per id. engram/1 fails the first test, and its bits carry nothing a model could use as a feature. Engram froze it on purpose (its D4) so Owl Academy’s chains stay readable.

The Lexicon keeps it only as an Owl-compatibility checksum, off by default for corpora and never an input (D4 here; Explainer 3). Claim L2 registers the collision rate: on the WordNet 3.0 lemma vocabulary (147,306 types in the pin), at least 1% of types share a key with another type; predicted 2–10%. Phase 1 found 52.6%: L2 is supported, and the prediction was wrong (Explainer 9). A 64-bit SHA-256-prefix key on the same types is the control and must give no collisions (Explainer 8).

Addresses are 5-adic, not semantic

The 28-letter address reads the 64-bit value in base 5, least significant digit first, each digit picking one of five children. Two values therefore share a k-letter prefix exactly when they agree mod 5k: their last k base-5 digits match. That is 5-adic closeness, of low digits, not of meaning; for hash-like engram values the tree distance is as random as the keys. In a chain a word’s letters also depend on the previous packet’s last letter, so only the 64-bit value is a stable id.

The design’s re-check of the code found one more limit: engram_core.encode always writes 28 letters, never uses the sixth root generator, and records no length. The Lexicon uses its own bijective id instead: 64 bits for words up to 27 letters, 59 bits up to 25 (Explainer 3).

What would give the prefix a meaning

Assign the ids by a hierarchy, and a shared prefix becomes a shared ancestor. That is the Lexicon’s address rule. WordNet’s own noun tree chooses the letters, and claim L3 tests whether the result is exact and invertible for all 82,115 noun synsets; Phase 1 found no failure on its Python side (Explainer 9). The lexicon address is the candidate for an engram/2 key, to be preregistered later in Engram’s own folder.

WordPiece, BERT’s subword tokenizer, splits each word greedily into the longest pieces found in a fixed vocabulary (a list of pieces, each with an id), marking continuations with “##”, so a rare word still gets ids. [CLS], [SEP] and [MASK] are reserved entries: sentence start, separator, and the hidden word a BERT learns to predict.

Nearly there: the softmax-then-Lorentz-centroid step (an attention-like average, in floats); one deterministic generator in JavaScript and Python (xoshiro128** seeded by splitmix32), so data splits and masked positions can be drawn identically everywhere; exact dyadic snapping; and the way of building, Python first, then a browser core with trace parity, claims registered before code.

A framing to drop

The site’s AI hub pages are tutorials with no runnable NLP code, and a PDF in research_papers argues that tokens should be compressed into quaternions. The repository’s own measurement disagrees: an exact quaternion chain costs about 130 bits per 64-bit packet. It stores; it does not compress.

05

Learning without gradients

The existing systems can learn, but only without gradients and only at toy scale. That decides where learning happens in the Lexicon.

Hyperbolic PolyStep is zeroth-order: it uses only values of the objective, never its gradient. One step works like this. Take a random block of factors. For each particle, score the 2·D corners of an orthoplex around it (one step each way along every axis). Weight the corners by a softmax or a Sinkhorn transport of their costs. Move each factor to the Lorentz centroid of its weighted corners. The step builds on polystep’s optimal-transport step (An T. Le, TMLR 2026) (how it works).

On the problem benchmarks flat did better. On MAX-3-SAT at n = 500 (B2), hyper3 reached 0.935 against flat3’s 0.962; on tree search (B3), flat3 beat hyper3 by 0.40–0.59 in success rate. The largest model, B4, is an encoder W in R32 plus 8 prototypes in H²: 48 numbers, 8 classes, 2×10⁵ evaluations, about 19 s per Python run. Its test accuracy: hyper3 0.325, flat3 0.334, polystep 0.336, random search 0.224, chance 0.125. Every learned method landed within about 0.014 of the others, hyper3 the weakest (the results).

The survey measured about 2,900 evaluations per second in Node. The smallest encoder the Lexicon plans, 1 million parameters on CPU, is more than 20,000 times the size of B4.

What gradient-free methods are good for

The literature agrees. MeZO runs zeroth-order SGD at inference memory cost: on one 80 GB A100 it trains a 30B-parameter model where backpropagation fits about 2.7B, and with OPT-13B it lands within 1% of Adam on 7 of 11 tasks. But it relies on pretraining and prompts. ZO-Bench and Evolution Strategies at scale tell the same story: they fine-tune, and none pretrains from scratch (MeZO; verified).

So PolyStep is kept for small jobs: heads, curvature and small adapters, in the literature’s terms. Decision D11 limits it to heads of at most a few hundred parameters. In the Lexicon it would tune the hierarchy head’s weight, threshold and temperature in the browser (Explainer 3). A fair comparison with MeZO and plain ZO-SGD, at equal evaluations on a fixed head, is a candidate claim for Phase 6. It will be registered by amendment before any code.

Where learning happens instead

Static embeddings are trained in Python float64 by a numpy trainer, the authority (D9), built in Phase 4: Nickel and Kiela’s Riemannian SGD in the Lorentz model (take the gradient, project it onto the sheet’s tangent plane, move along the sheet with the exponential map), and plain SGD for the Euclidean control. geoopt is a reported cross-check if torch installs. It is not installed here: PyPI’s Linux wheel brings the CUDA libraries, download.pytorch.org is blocked, and there is no GPU (4 CPU cores, 15 GB RAM; Explainer 6). Phase 4 did not run the cross-check: on 2026-11-06 the proxy refused download.pytorch.org, and a 60-digit decimal recomputation of one step and finite-difference gradients in the tests stand in its place (Explainer 12).

What C1 means for this system

Hyperbolic geometry clearly helps on explicit hierarchies, and modestly or not at all on general NLP. C1 tested optimisation and found no hyperbolic advantage. The Lexicon bets instead on representation (low-dimensional trees, which hyperbolic space has room for) and on certification (exactness, invertibility, identical results across engines). Every hyperbolic claim has a matched Euclidean control, including learned Euclidean tables at matched dimension (L6), and encoder-level gains are predicted small or null.

06

Exact against float

Hyperbolic space grows exponentially, and so do the numbers that describe deep points in it. Floats run out at a depth WordNet passes easily. Exact rationals do not run out, but they cost bits.

A float64 number keeps 53 significant bits, about 16 decimal digits, and rounds after every operation. An exact rational keeps a numerator and a denominator as integers of any size, and never rounds. In the H³ tree each step multiplies by cosh = 25/7 and sinh = 24/7, so a point at word length n has coordinates over 7ⁿ.

The chain’s cost is easy to see per packet. Forty packets took 5,204 bits (four components of 1,301 bits) to hold 2,560 bits of data:

64 data bits ~66 bits the exact chain adds

Unfolding grows roughly quadratically with chain length, so chains must be segmented. A 90-packet register, or one shard, is the natural unit (Explainer 4).

Why this bites on WordNet. The pinned noun spanning tree reaches depth 19 below entity.n.01. One node has 659 children, 3,291 nodes have more than five, and 396 have more than 25. Under the address rule, each child of a node with c children takes max(1, ⌈log₅ c⌉) letters. So the addresses of deep synsets run past depth 12, beyond which float decoding starts to fail. The design concludes that float64 is not certifiable at WordNet depth, and claim L4 registers that (Explainer 3).

The missing exact H² tree, and the one designed

No exact tree exists in H². The existing TreeEmbedding uses Math.cos and Math.sin. The Lexicon designs a new one on rational directions only: (1, 0), (3/5, ±4/5), (−7/25, ±24/25) and (−1, 0), with a boost of t = 11/13, so cosh = 145/24 and τ = ln 12.

A planning check, run in memory before any spike, found the following. Exact greedy decoding worked for 100/100 random words at depth 12 and 40/40 at depth 28. With the H³ step t = 3/4, the same directions decoded only 48/100 at depth 12. At depth 28 the new tree cost up to 217/143 bits (numerator/denominator) for random words and 229/188 in the worst case, against about 138/79 in H³. That is 1.5–2.4 times the bits. Spike S2 measures this properly; this page does not quote it (Explainer 3, Explainer 8).

Exactness moves the cost; it does not remove it

WordNet reconstruction is scored by MAP, mean average precision. For each node, rank every other node by distance, and measure how high its true neighbours come. Then average over all nodes; 1 is perfect. A combinatorial tree embedding reached a MAP of 0.989 in 2 dimensions, against 0.87 for learned Poincaré embeddings in 200. It needed almost 500 bits of precision, or 10 dimensions at 32 bits (Sala, De Sa, Gu & Ré 2018; verified; the 0.87 for Poincaré is Nickel & Kiela’s Table 1, noun-closure reconstruction at 200 dimensions, verified; Sala et al. score their 0.989 on the graph’s edges, so the pair is their framing, not a like-for-like comparison). Exact points pay that price in BigInt, as the 138 and 217 bits above show. Benchmarks will therefore compare at matched dimension and at matched bits.

08

The short answer

What have we built, for language? An exact, tested base for geometry, bookkeeping and integrity, and nothing that learns language.

Reuse as it is

The norm-5 tree and its LPS generators. The exact H³ tree with exact decoding. Q, the shared Rng, dyadic snapping and SHA-256. The verbatim shdccp/2 codec and Engram’s importer. The method: reference, parity, preregistration.

loaded unchanged, by relative path

Reuse, demoted

engram/1 as an Owl-compatibility checksum only. The fence and guard as descriptors. PolyStep for heads of a few hundred parameters. The float H² tree for drawing. Registers and the coupler stay out of the model: they are bookkeeping.

D4 · D11

Build new

A pinned normaliser and WordPiece tokenizer. The address rule and a bijective id code. An exact H² tree and exact ranking. A float64 trainer with Euclidean controls. Records and manifests for shards. An encoder only at Stage 2.
