Growing Language Biochains

Can language data be grown as biochains, the way BioChain-AI and Owl Academy grow them? Yes, but not as one growing chain: a whole lineage is capped at 60,000 bytes. A corpus becomes many unmodified shards, tied together by a manifest that only grows, described by typed records and checked by a stricter importer than the one in use today. This page walks through the design, with what the spikes measured, and ends with a lab that grows a shard from your text and breaks it.

01

Where this sits

This page expands answer 5 on the hub: could we grow biochains of language pre-processing data as SHD-CCP chains, as BioChain-AI grows biochains? Its focus node is the Records layer, where every table the front end produces becomes a hashed, signable commitment.

Phase 0 is analysis, seven measurement spikes and a preregistration. No grower, record, manifest or importer code exists yet; Phase 3 builds them and decides claims L8–L11 (README, roadmap). What does exist: spike S4 grew WordNet gloss shards against Owl Academy’s own code, and spike S7 signed and verified a record in BioChain-AI’s attestation format in two languages. Their numbers are spike results, run on data disjoint from the registered instances, and they decide nothing (S4 output, S7 output). The background comes from a read-only survey of Owl Academy at 7f45f35 and BioChain-AI at c975161, re-checked by an adversarial cross-check whose corrections win.

How to read the marks

exact: integers, bytes or hashes, identical in every engine. float: floating point, rounded at every step. REGISTERED: a claim in PREREGISTRATION.md, fixed and undecided. estimate: arithmetic on measured numbers, not a measurement. Timings come from the spikes’ “~” lines, which depend on this container (4 CPU cores, Node 22.23.4).

Biochain
One record that holds a UTF-8 text of at most 60,000 bytes, rebuilds it exactly, and carries hashes that commit to it. Owl Academy and BioChain-AI grow them in the browser.
Chunk, frame
The text is cut into chunks of 720 bytes. Each becomes a frame: a 64-bit packet, a 32-bit length and a residual.
Residual
The bits an order-2 rank coder writes for a chunk: for each byte, its rank among the bytes that followed the same two bytes before. Decoding replays the ranks, so the chunk comes back exactly.
Leaf, Merkle root
A leaf is one chunk’s SHA-256 hash. The Merkle root hashes the leaves in pairs, level by level, to one value: change any chunk and the root changes.
Typed commitment
A pipe-joined string with a kind and version first, such as GROWN/1|chainId|merkleRoot|…. Its SHA-256 is what gets signed.
Attestation
An ECDSA P-256 signature over an ATTEST/1|… payload that contains a commitment’s hash: it says which key committed to the record.
Shard, manifest
A shard is one biochain holding one slice of a corpus. A manifest is a record that commits to a list of shards, in order.
02

Biochains today

The biochain is the working SHD-CCP: the code Owl Academy and BioChain-AI actually run. Its pure codec is the same in both repositories and in Engram Mind’s Eye’s pinned copy, biochain_v1.js (Explainer 2).

How growBiochain grows one

  1. Cut. The text’s UTF-8 bytes are cut into 720-byte chunks. Above MAX_GROW_BYTES = 60000 it throws.
  2. Code. Each chunk goes through the order-2 rank coder, which starts fresh for every chunk and writes Elias-gamma codes, with an 8-bit escape for a byte not seen in its context.
  3. Crystallize. Each chunk is folded into one 64-bit word of form 9, a “crystal”: form (bits 63..60), even parity (59), spin, a 32-bit digest of the chunk read as four int8 quaternion codes, the chunk length in the 16-bit payload, and a byte-sum checksum in the last 8 bits.
  4. Hash. Leaf i is SHA-256 of i + "|" + sha256Hex(String.fromCharCode(...chunk)), over UTF-8. The leaves build a binary Merkle tree that duplicates the last node on odd levels.
  5. Weave. Each chunk’s crystal becomes a unit quaternion; their ordered product is the chiral weave, and the conjugates in reverse order give the mirror weave float.
  6. Name. chainId = "BC-" + SHA-256(merkleRoot + "|" + weaveChiral), first 24 hex digits. Frames are concatenated into one hex string, the shdccp/2 stream.
  7. Engrams (on by default). One 64-bit engram/1 key per word token, recomputed from the text on verification: the engramStream.

Publishing adds the owner, the grower, a lineage list and the certification block. In BioChain-AI that block is grownAttestation; in Owl Academy it is grownSeal. The content fields agree in all three codebases; the certification names and the Firebase projects differ.

What verifyIntegrity checks

Stopping at the first failure: each packet’s parity, decoding every residual, each leaf, the rebuilt Merkle root, the chiral weave, and the engram layer recomputed from the rebuilt text. Three things it does not do, which matter later (chapter 9): leaves and root are checked only when the fields are present; a packet’s crystal and checksum are never compared with the rebuilt chunk; and chainId, the mirror weave, segCuts, origBytes and value are never recomputed. It proves the record is consistent with itself, not who made it.

Authority: typed commitments

Who grew a chain, and where it went, is recorded by signed commitments, each a pipe-joined string built from the record’s own fields.

In BioChain-AI each is signed as an ATTEST/1 payload, ATTEST/1|keyId|keyVector|SHA256(commitment)|epochToken, with ECDSA P-256 over SHA-256 through WebCrypto, and checked against a keyRegistry of public keys. A key verifies only while it is active, and revocation is one way. Owl Academy signs the same commitments with its Minor Tome seals (SEAL/1|sealId|sealVector|contentHash|tickToken), and calls its transfer BIOMESH-XFER/1.

Where they live, and who can read them

A published biochain is one Firestore document, biochains/{chainId}, with the stream stored inline as hex. In both repositories’ firestore.rules, any signed-in user may read any biochain, and the stream decodes to plaintext: stored text is public to the whole tenant. On creation the rules check the grower and owner ids, not the record’s body, its chainId or its attestation, and nothing may be deleted. The Lexicon publishes no shards to Firestore this round (README, open choices).

03

The 60 KB lineage cap

A biochain can grow “further”, but only by regrowing. That puts a hard ceiling on a whole lineage, not on one step.

Both repositories set MAX_GROW_BYTES = 60000, with the comment “Firestore doc budget (stream stored inline)”. A published chain is frozen, so growth mints a new epoch. extendBiochain(parent, newText) verifies the parent, recreates its text, and calls growBiochain(parentText + newText). The child carries parentChainId, parentMerkleRoot, epoch + 1 and addedBytes, sealed by GROWTH/1. Nothing is appended: every epoch regrows everything, so its cost grows with the whole text, and the whole text must stay within 60,000 bytes.

epoch 1 + epoch 2, regrown + 3 cap
A correction that shaped the design

Two of the survey’s reports said larger corpora could grow through many GROWTH/1 epochs; a third said a lineage can never pass 60 KB. The cross-check read biomesh.js again and sided with the third: growBiochain throws above the cap, and extendBiochain passes it the whole text. So corpus growth needs many independent shards.

04

Shards

Decision D8: shards of at most 60,000 UTF-8 bytes, in unmodified shdccp/2 with the JavaScript codec’s hashing. A shard is an ordinary biochain, so every existing reader accepts it as it is.

Unmodified matters. Owl Academy, BioChain-AI and Engram Mind’s Eye already verify shdccp/2; any change of bytes would cut the shards off from them. So everything the Lexicon adds lives beside the shard, in records (chapter 5), and everything that would change bytes is only proposed, under a future shdccp/3 (Explainer 2).

What spike S4 measured

S4 grew the 22 verb and 33 adjective gloss shards, and 8 synthetic ones, with a spike-only synchronous grower: biochain_v1.js’s verbatim codec, the orbital core’s SHA-256 over UTF-8, and Owl’s leaf, Merkle, weave and chainId rules restated line for line. It then ran Owl Academy’s own growBiochain, unchanged, on the same texts.

The quirks a Python port must copy

  • Leaf hashing. String.fromCharCode turns each byte into a code point and SHA-256 reads the UTF-8 of that string, so every byte from 0x80 up is hashed as two bytes. BioChain-AI’s Python hashes binary, and the two cannot verify each other’s chains.
  • The 0x80 byte. Written raw, as the JavaScript does: 1.4131% of gloss chunks have a crystal holding one (64 of 4,529; analytic 1.5534%), and 39 of the 55 gloss shards have at least one.
  • The float weave. V8’s Math.hypot algorithm and an exact toFixed(12) (chapter 7).
Claim L8 REGISTERED

Language shards are Owl-valid biochains, field for field, in JS and Python: our synchronous JS grower and our Python port each give the record Owl Academy’s own growBiochain gives (BioChain-AI’s gives the same); Owl’s and BioChain-AI’s verifyIntegrity accept every record; importBiochain accepts every record and recreates the text; the derived layers and the LPREP/1 strings agree in Python and JS. Instances: C0’s 148 shards and 20 synthetic ones from Rng(1000 + i), each grown with engrams on and off. Controls that must fail: a Python grower with the kernel’s 0x80 normalisation; a grower that hashes leaves over binary bytes; one altered byte must change its leaf and root. S4’s 126/126 is a spike result on verb, adjective and synthetic shards, not this claim’s.

Kinds, not Form values. Decision D7 keeps the namespace in record kinds, never in a Form nibble: every one of the 16 Form values is already claimed by some consumer (Explainer 2), and a biochain document has no type field (publishBiochain drops meta.kind). Each Lexicon record therefore carries {kind, spec: 'hyperbolic-lexicon/0.1', compat: 'biomesh-js'}.

Derived layers are recomputed, not stored. Token ids and addresses are never written per token. They follow the engramStream precedent: a layer that is a pure function of the rebuilt text and pinned tables, which a verifier recomputes and compares by hash. The record holds only the id stream’s SHA-256 (LEB128 varints, one per token) and addrTableRoot. That works only because the normaliser, the tokenizer and the address table are pinned and deterministic, which is what claims L1 and L3 test (Explainer 3). It also avoids the engram layer’s cost: 8 bytes (16 hex digits) for every word token, while S4’s gloss text averaged 6.7 bytes a token, so the layer is larger than the text it describes and more than halves a shard’s value (chapter 10).

06

The manifest

Many shards need one commitment to the whole list, in order, that can grow without being rebuilt and that answers two questions cheaply: is this shard in the corpus, and is today’s corpus an extension of last month’s?

LCORPUS/1 is an RFC 6962 Merkle tree, the one Certificate Transparency logs use, over SHA-256(LPREP/1) in shard order:

  • Leaf hash = SHA-256(0x00 ‖ entry). Node hash = SHA-256(0x01 ‖ left ‖ right). The prefixes keep a leaf from ever passing as a node.
  • Split rule. A list of n > 1 entries splits at k, the largest power of two below n: the left subtree takes the first k, the right the rest. Nothing is duplicated.
  • Inclusion proof. To show entry i is in a tree of N, give the sibling hashes along its path, about log₂ N of them; the verifier rebuilds the root.
  • Consistency proof. To show a tree of N extends a smaller tree of m (the first m entries unchanged), give a few subtree hashes from which both roots can be rebuilt. That is what makes LGROW/1 append-only.

Why not the shdccp/2 rule

The biochain rule hashes hex strings in pairs and, on an odd level, copies the last node before pairing. So [a, b, c] pairs as (a, b), (c, c), and [a, b, c, c] pairs as (a, b), (c, c) too: two different lists, one root. This is the CVE-2012-2459 class.

shdccp/2
[a, b, c]    → H(H(a b) H(c c))
[a, b, c, c] → H(H(a b) H(c c))
               the same root

RFC 6962, with h(x) = H(0 ‖ x)
[a, b, c]    → H(1 ‖ H(1 ‖ h(a) ‖ h(b)) ‖ h(c))
[a, b, c, c] → H(1 ‖ H(1 ‖ h(a) ‖ h(b)) ‖ H(1 ‖ h(c) ‖ h(c)))
               different roots

Spike S4 checked every K from 1 to 200. Under the shdccp/2 rule, a list and the same list with its last element repeated had equal roots at K = 3, 5, 7, …, 199: every odd K ≥ 3, 99 values. K = 1 is safe, because a one-leaf root is the leaf itself and is never hashed. Repeating the last two elements collided at K = 6, 10, 14, …, 198 (49 values). RFC 6962 roots of the same lists were equal at 0 of 200.

Inside one shard the damage is limited, because the stream fixes how many leaves there are. A manifest is different: a root alone must fix the list it commits to. So shards keep their shdccp/2 root for compatibility, and every list the Lexicon commits to (shards, address-table rows) uses RFC 6962 (Lemma 15 note; the lab below shows both rules side by side).

Claim L9(a) REGISTERED

LCORPUS/1 is an RFC 6962 tree over SHA-256(LPREP/1) in shard order. Every inclusion proof verifies for its own leaf and position and fails for any other; every consistency proof from a smaller prefix size m verifies, so LGROW/1 is append-only; every single alteration (replace, delete, insert, adjacent swap, duplicate-last, truncation with the old root kept) is detected; JS and Python give identical roots and proofs. Instances: the manifest over C0 (K = 148) and synthetic lists with K ∈ {1, 2, 3, 7, 84, 200, 1000, 1001}. The control that must fail: the shdccp/2 rule must collide at every odd K ≥ 3 among them.

07

The weave

A Merkle root says which chunks a chain holds. The weave is a second summary, built to change when chunks swap places. Today it is computed in floats, and that makes it fragile across languages.

The float weave

Each chunk’s 32-bit crystal is read as four signed bytes s, made into (0.5 + s₀/254, s₁/127, s₂/127, s₃/127), and normalised with Math.hypot. The weave multiplies these unit quaternions in order, renormalising after each step, and prints each component with toFixed(12) float. Because chainId hashes that string, a rounding difference that reaches the twelfth decimal changes the chain’s name.

Spike S4 ported the weave to Python two ways and compared both with Owl’s JavaScript on 1,000 synthetic shards. A faithful port (V8’s Math.hypot algorithm, exact toFixed(12)) differed on 0 of 1,000. A naive port (Python’s math.hypot and '%.12f') wrote a different weave string on 2 of 1,000. The cause was hypot: Python’s differed from V8’s on 39,408 of 127,152 calls (30.9928%), while the formatting differed on 0 of 8,000 components.

The exact norm-5 weave

The Lexicon proposes an exact replacement, built from code Engram Mind’s Eye already ships. Take the first 8 bytes of each leaf hash as a 64-bit value. engram_core.chainQuaternion writes each value as a 28-letter word of the norm-5 tree (each letter one of the integer quaternions 1±2i, 1±2j, 1±2k) and multiplies the whole chain out into one integer quaternion exact. Its norm is exactly 528K for K leaves, and right division (engram_core.unfold) reads the values back, in order (Explainer 1).

Claim L9(b) REGISTERED

Each shard’s exact norm-5 weave, engram_core.chainQuaternion over the first 8 bytes of each leaf hash in order, has norm exactly 528K, unfolds in order to those prefixes, equals the ordered product of its 7 segment sub-weaves exactly, and has identical components in JS and Python, on every C0 shard. The control that must fail: an XOR summary of the same prefixes must miss at least one adjacent swap that the weave detects.

08

Two tiers

Every signed-in user can read every biochain, and a stream decodes to plaintext. Much language data may not be shared that way. So the design has two tiers: the text travels only when its licence allows; otherwise only commitments do.

Tier S · the stream

Plaintext travels losslessly in the shdccp/2 stream, with its leaves, for text whose licence allows redistribution: WordNet with its notice kept (licence), UD English EWT (CC BY-SA 4.0), BLiMP (CC BY), public-domain Gutenberg texts, and the team’s own text. Anyone holding a shard can rebuild and check it.

public recomputation

Tier P · public commitments

Restricted corpora (BNC, OpenSubtitles, CHILDES/TalkBank, gated EWoK) publish only hashes, roots, counts, SPDX ids, metrics and receipts. Their streams stay on the holder’s disk, or in object storage keyed by root. SNOMED CT is excluded; EWoK appears by hash only.

attestation plus audit

Salted commitments

A bare hash of a known text can be confirmed by anyone who hashes the same text. So a Tier-P shard’s public LPREP/1 carries saltedRoot = SHA-256('LPREP-SALT/1'|salt|merkleRoot|chainId), with a 32-byte secret salt per shard, and omits chainId. Without the salt, guessing the text confirms nothing.

Challenge audits

Nobody else can recompute a Tier-P shard, so it is checked by challenge. The auditor derives k shard indices from SHA-256(LCORPUS root | ‘audit’ | n); the holder reveals those shards, their salts and their inclusion proofs, privately. If a fraction f of the shards were bad, the audit misses all of them with probability (1 − f)k:

What Tier P is, and is not

It is attestation plus audit, not public recomputation, and the design says so plainly. A signature proves who committed a record. It does not prove that the licence holds or that the data is correct.

Contamination receipts. LCORPUS/1 also commits to a receipt: the 13-gram SHA-256 sets of the evaluation items, intersected with the training id streams. Only the counts are published. The full licence table is in Explainer 7.

The hardened import

The verifier in use today proves that a record agrees with itself. Spike S4 measured how much that leaves open. The Lexicon’s importer closes it, and reports two verdicts instead of one.

What verifyIntegrity accepts

Residual aliasing. The last two rows need a word. When the decoder reads a rank larger than the number of bytes seen in a context, it reads the next 8 bits as a raw byte, whatever the rank was. So an escape code has aliases: several bit patterns decode to the same chunk, and leaves, root, weave and chainId all commit to decoded bytes only. S4 flipped every single bit of frame 0’s residual in verb shards 00–03: 689 of 19,400 flips (3.5515%) decoded to the same 720 bytes (681 inside a raw-byte rank code, 8 in the last byte’s padding, 0 elsewhere). A re-encode check (encode the decoded chunk and require the stored residual, byte for byte) rejected 689 of 689. S4 also altered one byte of each verb shard’s stream; importBiochain rejected 21 of 22 copies, and the one it accepted decodes to the same bytes.

The checks

  1. Packet parity, as today.
  2. Decode, then re-encode each chunk and require the stored residual, byte for byte: only the canonical encoding passes.
  3. Re-crystallize each chunk and require the stored packet: crystal and checksum are checked at last.
  4. Leaves and root mandatory, recomputed with the JavaScript rule.
  5. Both weaves and chainId recomputed.
  6. The derived layers (the V0 id stream, addrTableRoot) and the exact weave recomputed; the LPREP/1 commitment rebuilt from them.
  7. The ATTEST/1 signature verified against a pinned snapshot of the key registry, refusing revoked keys and key-vector mismatches; P1363 converted to DER for Python, and back with r and s left-padded.
  8. Manifest proofs: the shard’s inclusion in LCORPUS/1, and the consistency of each LGROW/1.

Self-consistent

Steps 1–6 and 8: the record, its derived layers and its place in the manifest agree with one another. Anyone can check this, but anyone can also produce it, by growing an altered text.

Authentic

Step 7: a registered, unrevoked key committed to exactly this record. Today nothing in Engram’s import path calls verifyGrown, and the Firestore rules do not check attestations.

Engram’s importBiochain is not changed; the Lexicon’s importer wraps it.

Claim L10 REGISTERED

The lexicon importer, in JS (WebCrypto) and Python (cryptography), accepts every clean attested record and rejects every record in the registered tamper set, naming the failing check; its verdicts are reported separately as self-consistent and authentic, and are identical in both languages; Owl’s verifyIntegrity accepts the classes that only the lexicon importer’s checks catch. Instances: 50 C0 shards, each LPREP/1 attested with a P-256 key generated per run, and 9 tamper classes of 50 records each: T1 leaves and root removed, T2 a crystal changed with parity re-fixed, T3 an LPREP/1 field changed, T4 a revoked key, T5 a valid attestation of a different shard, T6 a Tier-P commitment with a wrong salt, T7 two frames swapped, T8 chainId altered, T9 a residual bit flipped that still decodes the same; 450 tampered records and 50 clean. Controls that must fail: Owl’s verifyIntegrity must accept at least one T1, T2, T8 and T9 record; Python without the P1363-to-DER conversion must reject every valid signature.

Claim L11 REGISTERED

A holder verifies a full C0 shard with every hardened check of L10 (and the V0 id stream and addrTableRoot recomputed) in a median of at most 250 ms in Node, single-threaded, on this container; a 1,000-shard corpus therefore verifies in under about 5 minutes. Instances: the 147 C0 shards of at least 59,000 bytes, 3 timed repetitions each, the first discarded. Supported if the upper end of the median’s bootstrap interval is ≤ 250 ms; refuted if the lower end exceeds 500 ms; inconclusive otherwise. The control: a run with the attestation step skipped must be faster. Predicted: a median of about 50–150 ms.

Adding up what the spikes timed, the codec part, the re-encode and the attestation come to about 41.0 + 23.5 + 0.2 ≈ 65 ms a shard estimate. The id stream, addrTableRoot and the LPREP/1 rebuild are not timed yet: Phase 1 built the tokenizer and the addresses in Python only, and L11 is timed in Node, whose core comes in Phase 2. That is why L11 sets a threshold with headroom and predicts a range, not a number.

The grower follows the steps of spike S4’s synchronous grower: biochain_v1.js for encoding, crystals, framing and the weave quaternions, OrbitalCore.sha256 over UTF-8 for leaves, root and chainId. S4 found that grower equal to Owl’s growBiochain on 126/126 records; this page’s copy was checked against the three Owl-grown records in Engram’s bridges_v1.json, not separately registered. The left box is EngramCore.verifyBiochain, which repeats Owl’s verifyIntegrity step for step. The right box sketches the codec checks L10 adds; it is not the Phase 3 importer, and the derived layers, the LPREP/1 commitment, the signature and the manifest proofs are not in it. “Flip one byte” flips the top bit of one byte of frame 0’s residual (by default the middle byte, as S4 did). The Merkle panel uses the list L = SHA-256(“leaf 0”), SHA-256(“leaf 1”), …: the shdccp/2 rule pairs their hex strings, RFC 6962 hashes their 32 bytes with the 0x00 and 0x01 prefixes. Generated texts are repetitive word lists, so their sizes and values are not those of real text; S4’s values are for WordNet glosses.

Try it
  • Flip one byte: the decoder either runs out of bits (“residual truncated”) or rebuilds different bytes (“leaf 0 mismatch”). Slide the byte along: now and then a flip lands inside an escape’s rank code, which still reads as an escape, and the chunk decodes the same. Then Owl’s steps accept, and only the re-encode check objects.
  • Delete leaves and root, change a crystal, alter chainId: Owl’s steps still accept. These are S4’s T1, T2 and T8.
  • Three chunks, then swap frames 0 and 1: rejected at leaf 0, because the stored leaves fix the order. The shard’s own three leaves also show the duplicate-last collision in the Merkle panel.
  • 60,001 B: the error growBiochain throws. One byte over is the lineage cap.
  • Non-ASCII: each byte from 0x80 up is hashed as two bytes in the leaf, the quirk a Python port must copy.

Into Engram Mind’s Eye

Every Lexicon shard is a shdccp/2 biochain, so the bridge into Engram Mind’s Eye already exists. What it lacks is a key whose bits mean something; that is the longer bridge.

EngramCore.importBiochain runs verifyBiochain (Owl’s verifyIntegrity, step for step), then checks that Engram’s own engram stream of the rebuilt text equals the record’s, and brings the text in as one turn of words, tokenized as biomesh.js tokenizes them (Engram’s bridges). S4 measured it accepting all 22 verb shards, engrams off and on, with the text recreated byte for byte; with engrams off its median was 170.6 ms a shard. It computes Engram’s own engram stream of the text on every import, and that layer alone took a median 130.5 ms a shard in S4. It checks no signature.

Now

Shards import unchanged through importBiochain; claim L8(c) registers it on all of C0. The Lexicon’s importer wraps it with the hardened checks and the attestation.
