CHAPTER 1

The big idea

Most calculators treat a sum as an instant, invisible event. This engine slows a calculation down and gives it a shape: a journey inward through five nested rings, ending in an answer that is exact rather than "very close".

Why ordinary computers get 0.1 + 0.2 wrong

Almost every computer stores decimal numbers in a binary format called IEEE-754 floating point. A 64-bit "double" keeps about 15 to 17 significant digits (53 binary digits, the mantissa) and throws the rest away.

That works well for most things, but some everyday decimals have no exact binary form. One tenth is the classic example: in base 2 it repeats forever, 0.000110011001100…, much as one third repeats forever in base 10 (0.333…). The computer has to cut it off, and the tiny leftover error shows up when you add:

0.1 + 0.2 = 0.30000000000000004  // what a standard double gives
0.1 + 0.2 = 0.3  // what this engine gives

A second failure is the precision ceiling. Try 9.0000000000000005 + 0.30000008 on a normal calculator. The tiny …0005 at the end is beyond the 53-bit limit, so it silently disappears and you get 9.30000008. The engine keeps every digit: 9.3000000800000005.

The engine's answer, in two layers

Layer 1: exact fractions

Every number you type is turned into a fraction of two whole numbers, for example 0.1 becomes 1/10. Whole numbers of any size can be stored exactly (JavaScript's BigInt), so adding, subtracting, multiplying and dividing fractions never loses anything. The answer is converted back to decimals only at the very end.

Layer 2: a geometric picture

The engine shows that pipeline as motion through space. Five ring-shaped surfaces (tori, like doughnuts) sit inside each other. A glowing orb starts on the outer ring and spirals inward, one ring per stage, until it reaches the core and the answer "latches".

In plain words

Think of the five rings as five rooms in a factory, from loading dock to final inspection. Your numbers come in at the outer room as raw material, get lined up, combined, trimmed of waste, and leave the inner room as a finished, exact answer. The orb's path is the tour of the factory.

The vocabulary you'll meet

The engine uses the language of the research paper behind it: manifold, soliton, holonomy, spinor. You don't need any of it to use the calculator, and each term is explained where it first matters (and again in the glossary). As a first translation:

CHAPTER 2

Quick start: your first calculation

Five minutes, no theory needed. Open the engine in another tab and follow along.

  1. Look around. The coloured rings in the middle are the five tiers. Drag with the mouse (or one finger) to rotate the view, scroll (or pinch) to zoom, right-drag (or two fingers) to pan. The crosshair button at the bottom left recentres the camera.
  2. Open the TERMINAL. In the left panel, click 2. TERMINAL. This is the calculator keypad.
  3. Load the classic test. Click IEEE Test: 0.1 + 0.2. The expression appears in the display.
  4. Press RESOLVE MANIFOLD (=). The right panel switches to REGISTER. A pink orb appears on the outer red ring (T4) and a red diamond marks where it started.
  5. Watch the five stages. The orb winds around each ring, then jumps inward. Each jump drops a new coloured diamond: amber (T3), green (T2), purple (T1), blue (T0). The Register lists the stages and ticks them off. Stages 3 and 4 run more slowly on purpose; that's where the answer is "trimmed" and "latched".
  6. Read the result. When the last stage finishes, the display shows 0.3 exactly, a message confirms the result, and an explanation card appears in the Register (Node A = 1/10, Node B = 1/5, Collapse = 3/10).
  7. Try the ceiling test. Back in TERMINAL, click 64-Bit Ceiling Test and resolve again. The answer keeps all 16 decimal places.
  8. Inspect what happened. In the Register, click any stage card (or the S0–S4 pills) to replay that stage's 30 frames, and click a frame row to see its measurements. Then drag the bottom Frame Scrubber to move the orb back and forth along its path.
  9. Make a fingerprint. Click Hash to Engram (next to Reset Before Calc). The left panel jumps to 3. ENGRAM and shows your result as a 64-bit tile.
Try it

Type your own expression with the keypad, for example 1/3, 2/7*7 or (1+2)*3. Switch to SCIENTIFIC for sin, cos, √, ^, π and brackets. Press C to clear. Not sure what to type? Use the exact-vs-float lab in this manual first.

Good to know

The answer is worked out the instant you press =; the animation then walks you through the five stages and releases the answer at the end. At the default speed (0.10x) one calculation takes about six to seven seconds. Keep Reset Before Calc ticked if you want every run of the same expression to trace exactly the same path.

CHAPTER 4

The five tiers

Everything in the engine hangs off five tori. Their sizes and winding speeds aren't arbitrary: each follows a simple formula in the tier number n (0 for the core, 4 for the outside).

What a torus is, and how a path winds around it

A torus is a doughnut shape. It has two radii: the major radius R (from the centre of the doughnut to the middle of its tube) and the minor radius r (the thickness of the tube). A point on it is fixed by two angles: θ goes the long way round the ring, φ goes round the tube.

$$x = (R + r\cos\varphi)\cos\theta,\qquad y = (R + r\cos\varphi)\sin\theta,\qquad z = r\sin\varphi$$

If you tie the two angles together as φ = W·θ, a point travelling once round the ring wraps round the tube W times, drawing a spiral like the stripe on a candy cane bent into a circle. W is the tier's winding number. The coloured helix tubes on each torus in the 3D view are exactly these spirals.

The four ratios that tie them together

  1. The winding invariant, WΣ = 60. Add the winding numbers: 8 + 10 + 12 + 14 + 16 = 60. This is the WΣ = 60 badge in the header, and 60 is also the engine's base pulse rate (60 pulses per second at nominal speed).
  2. The 2:1 inward acceleration. Every stage takes the same number of pulses (30), but inner tiers wrap more often: 8 wraps on T4, 16 on T0. So the spin rate doubles from outside to inside, ω0/ω4 = 16/8 = 2, like a skater spinning faster as they pull their arms in. The tier frequencies f = 6W run 48, 60, 72, 84, 96 Hz.
  3. Quadratic compression. The gaps between rings shrink in equal steps (50, 40, 30, 20: a constant "second difference" of 10, which is what a quadratic formula produces). Overall the ring shrinks from 155 to 15, a factor of 31/3 ≈ 10.33.
  4. The π/6 handshake. The middle tier T2 has W = 12, which splits a full turn into twelve 30° (π/6) sectors. 30° becomes the "gear tooth" that the outer and inner tiers have to agree on (chapter 10).

Why the tiers alternate between flat and upright

Neighbouring tiers are orthogonal: T4, T2 and T0 lie flat, T3 and T1 stand upright. A calculation moving inward therefore has to "fold" through 90° at every stage. The paper calls the jump between tiers an orthogonal geodesic plunge; on screen it's the moment the orb leaves one ring and swoops onto the next. Because consecutive rings are perpendicular, no two neighbours ever overlap, and each stage begins in a fresh plane.

What else is drawn on each tier

  • Dual-helix braid tubes. Six spirals per tier: three starting phases (0°, 120°, 240°, the C3 triad) times two directions (forward, blue, and reverse, amber, the C2 chirality). Together they form a braid.
  • Square particle fields. Sixteen square particles ride each of those six spirals, so 96 per tier and 480 in all. They flow at the tier's frequency whenever the engine is running, which makes the inward speed-up visible.
  • Orthogonal radial lines. A faint line from the centre to every particle, a reminder that every point is measured from the core.
  • The scalar core. The white sphere at the centre. It glows blue when the system is in lock and turns red when the clutch slips or drift is injected (chapter 11).
  • A polar grid lies faintly in the background for orientation.
CHAPTER 5

The 30-node clock

The engine replaces "frames per second" with a clock built from the geometry itself. Each ring is divided into 30 nodes, and the pattern those nodes follow is what the paper calls the intrinsic topological clock.

Thirty nodes, 12° apart

Each tier is cut into 30 equal steps of 12°: node k sits at θk = 2πk/30. The engine labels them F1/30 … F30/30. Two repeating patterns run across them at once:

  • C3, the triadic phase. Every three nodes cycle through 0°, 120° and 240° (k mod 3). This keeps the path balanced: no direction is favoured.
  • C2, chiral parity. Nodes alternate between the forward braid (+1) and the reverse braid (−1) (k mod 2).

Together they form a 6-node pattern (C3×C2), and 30 nodes hold exactly five copies of it. That's what the label |C₃ × C₂| × 5 Tiers on the Active Node card refers to.

The Cesium-133 half-cycle

The international definition of the second is 9,192,631,770 oscillations of a caesium-133 atom. At the engine's base rate of 60 pulses per second, one pulse lasts

$$\frac{9{,}192{,}631{,}770}{60} = 153{,}210{,}529.5 \text{ caesium cycles}$$

That trailing .5 is the parity residual, shown as φ_p on the Parity State card. After an odd number of pulses there's half a cycle left over (φ_p = 0.5); after an even number it cancels (φ_p = 0.0). At node 30 that integer closure coincides with a full 360° turn and the end of the fifth C3×C2 pattern:

$$153{,}210{,}529.5 \times 30 = 4{,}596{,}315{,}885 \text{ cycles, exactly}$$

So one 30-node lap takes half a second at nominal speed and contains a whole number of caesium cycles. That is the gate the engine waits for before letting a calculation move inward to the next tier.

Lab · the 30-node dial

Colour = C3 phase (cyan 0°, amber 120°, fuchsia 240°). Filled = forward braid (+1), hollow = reverse braid (−1). Drag the slider to step through the nodes.

From 30 nodes to 150 pulses to 720 frames

  • A calculation visits all five tiers, 30 nodes each: 150 pulses. At the nominal 60 pulses per second that's 0.5 s per stage and 2.5 s in total.
  • On screen it runs slower so you can follow it. During a calculation the clock runs at 6× the speed slider for stages 0–2 and 2.5× for stages 3–4. At the default 0.10x that's roughly 0.8 s per outer stage and 2 s per inner stage: about 6–7 seconds in all.
  • The same journey can also be counted as 720 frames, two full turns of 360°. The paper calls this the holonomic or spinor view. Spinors are mathematical objects that only return to their starting state after 720°, not 360° (the "belt trick" is the everyday example). The bottom scrubber can count in either unit (chapter 15).
CHAPTER 6

The calculation trace

The fuchsia orb and its comet trail show where the calculation "is". Here is exactly how it starts, moves and finishes.

Go deeper

The Calculation Tracer is a companion page that follows the orb stage by stage with live diagrams: the entry and exit gates of each tier, the V1–V4 TQF polygons and tension/torsion in motion, whether the engine uses sine and cosine at all, and the computational shortcuts it takes.

Where it starts

With Reset Before Calc ticked, pressing = resets the clock, the pulse counter and the manifold's rotation to zero, so the same expression always traces the same path. The orb starts on T4 at θ = 0, φ = 0:

$$P_{\text{start}} = \big((R_4 + r_4)\,,\;0\,,\;0\big) = (155 + 12,\;0,\;0) = (167,\;0,\;0)$$

A crimson-rose octahedron, the first checkpoint diamond, is dropped there to mark the start of Stage 0.

Spinning around a tier

During each stage the orb winds around the current tier along its helix (φ = Wθ), so it circles the inner tiers faster than the outer ones. Each animation frame it moves a quarter of the way toward its target point (lerp(target, 0.25)), which smooths the motion into a flowing comet trail.

Jumping between tiers

When the clock reaches node 30 on the current tier (the parity gate from chapter 5), the stage advances:

  1. A new checkpoint diamond is dropped where the orb is, coloured for the stage it's entering: amber (Stage 1, T3), green (Stage 2, T2), purple (Stage 3, T1), blue (Stage 4, T0). Each diamond wears a white halo ring.
  2. The orb's target switches to the next tier inward, which is turned 90° from the one it's leaving.
  3. Because the orb eases toward its new target, it sweeps across the gap in a curve rather than teleporting: that curve is the inward bridge you see in the trail.
  4. Entering Stage 3 the engine deliberately slows down (the clock drops from 6× to 2.5×). This is the pruning stage, and the slower pace lets you see the last two tiers clearly.

Where it finishes

The calculation is complete when the clock closes node 30 on T0, the fifth and last parity gate: 5 stages × 30 nodes = 150 pulses, or 720° of the holonomic cycle. At that moment the engine:

  • writes the result into the display and the Register, and shows the explanation card;
  • stops the animation (unless FREE SPIN is on, in which case the rings keep turning);
  • pops up a message such as Manifold Resolution Complete: 0.1 + 0.2 = 0.3.
Two different paths

While a calculation runs, the trail records where the orb actually went. When you scrub afterwards (with the Frame Scrubber, the Hub's trajectory scan, the stage cards or +1 Pulse), the engine draws an idealised version instead, the canonical trajectory: one point per node, each blended 38% of the way toward that node's position. Both describe the same T4→T0 journey; the canonical one is tidier for inspection.

CHAPTER 7

Exact arithmetic, stage by stage

What the engine computes is exact rational arithmetic: every value is a fraction n/d of two whole numbers with no size limit. Here is the pipeline, mapped onto the five stages.

Stage 0 on T4: turning decimals into exact fractions

Each number is read as text, never as a float. A number with k digits after the decimal point becomes (all its digits) / 10k, then is reduced by the greatest common divisor (GCD), found with Euclid's 2,300-year-old algorithm:

$$\gcd(a, b) = \gcd(b,\; a \bmod b), \qquad \gcd(a, 0) = a$$

Examples: 0.1 → 1/10. 0.2 → 2/10 → 1/5. 9.0000000000000005 has 16 decimals:

$$\frac{90{,}000{,}000{,}000{,}000{,}005}{10{,}000{,}000{,}000{,}000{,}000} \xrightarrow{\;\div 5\;} \frac{18{,}000{,}000{,}000{,}000{,}001}{2{,}000{,}000{,}000{,}000{,}000}$$

Nothing has been rounded. The fraction is the number, exactly.

Stage 1 on T3: putting operations in order

The expression is reordered with Dijkstra's shunting-yard algorithm so that operations happen in the right order: brackets first, then ^, then * and /, then + and -. Powers group from the right, so 2^3^2 means 29 = 512, as in standard maths. Conceptually this is also where pairs of fractions are brought onto a common denominator:

$$\frac{1}{10} = \frac{1\times5}{10\times5} = \frac{5}{50},\qquad \frac{1}{5} = \frac{1\times10}{5\times10} = \frac{10}{50}$$

For 0.1 + 0.2 this stage produces 5/50 + 10/50 = 15/50.

The square root uses Newton's method on whole numbers, starting from S/2 and repeating until the guess stops shrinking:

$$x_{k+1} = \left\lfloor \frac{x_k + \lfloor S / x_k \rfloor}{2} \right\rfloor, \qquad S = \left\lfloor \frac{n \cdot 10^{40}}{d} \right\rfloor$$

The sine and cosine are the polynomials

$$\sin a \approx a - \frac{a^3}{6} + \frac{a^5}{120}, \qquad \cos a \approx 1 - \frac{a^2}{2} + \frac{a^4}{24}$$

computed with fractions, so they use no floating-point hardware at all. (See chapter 8 for how accurate this is.)

Stage 3 on T1: pruning (TQF collinear collapse)

Every intermediate fraction is reduced to lowest terms by dividing top and bottom by their GCD:

$$c = \gcd(|N|, D), \qquad \frac{N}{D} \to \frac{N / c}{D / c}$$

For our example, gcd(15, 50) = 5, so 15/50 → 3/10. The paper connects this to geometry: a common factor carries no new information, just as three points on a straight line (where the TQF area is zero, chapter 9) carry no new curvature. Both get "pruned". This is the stage where the engine slows down.

Stage 4 on T0: the latch (writing out the decimals)

The final fraction N/D is turned back into decimal digits by long division, exactly as you'd do on paper:

  1. The whole-number part is N ÷ D (rounded down); the remainder is N mod D.
  2. While the remainder isn't zero: multiply it by 10, the next digit is (remainder × 10) ÷ D, and the new remainder is what's left over. Each repetition is one "base-10 wrap".
  3. Stop when the remainder is zero, or after 48 decimal places (then ... is added to show the digits go on).

For 3/10: 30 ÷ 10 = 3 remainder 0, so the answer is 0.3, with no binary error anywhere. For the ceiling test, every one of the 16 decimal places comes out: 9.3000000800000005.

How the computation and the animation line up

The arithmetic is done all at once when you press =. In fact every +, -, * and / is reduced to lowest terms as it happens, so the "align, combine, prune" steps all happen inside each single operation. The five stages are the engine's way of showing that pipeline at human speed. The result is held back until the animation reaches the core, then latched.

CHAPTER 9

Between the manifolds: quadrance and the TQF

The space between the rings isn't empty in this model. At every node the engine measures how the calculation point sits between its two neighbouring tiers, using rational trigonometry.

Quadrance instead of distance

Ordinary distance needs a square root, d = √(Δx² + Δy² + Δz²), and square roots are usually irrational. Rational trigonometry (developed by mathematician Norman Wildberger) uses the quadrance instead, which is just the distance squared:

$$Q = d^2 = \Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2 + \Delta z^2$$

If the coordinates are fractions, so is the quadrance. No square root, no rounding.

The four vertices

For each node the engine takes four points:

V1 · Pnode

The active node on the current tier (cyan).

V2 · Pupper

A point on the next tier outward (amber).

V3 · Plower

A point on the next tier inward (emerald).

V4 · P4

The inferred 4th "equilibrium" point (fuchsia), worked out from the other three.

The outermost and innermost tiers have only one neighbour each, so they borrow the next one along: T4 is bounded by T3 and T2, T0 by T2 and T1. The Register shows this as Bounds: T3 & T2 and so on. The three points are spread round the tube by the C3 phases (0°, 120°, 240°) and offset slightly along the ring, so they always form a genuine 3D triangle.

Three quadrances and two ratios

$$Q_1 = \lVert V_1 - V_2 \rVert^2,\qquad Q_2 = \lVert V_1 - V_3 \rVert^2,\qquad Q_3 = \lVert V_2 - V_3 \rVert^2$$
  • Projective quadrance \( \hat{Q} = \dfrac{Q_1 + Q_2}{Q_1 + Q_2 + Q_3} \), always between 0 and 1. It measures how the node is "stretched" between its two neighbours: near 1, the node is far from both compared with how far apart they are.
  • Inverse projective quadrance \( \hat{Q}^{-1} = 1/\hat{Q} \), always above 1. The paper reads it as the local compression acting on the path as the gaps between rings narrow.

The Triple Quad Formula and the area remainder

Three points lie on one straight line exactly when their quadrances satisfy the Triple Quad Formula:

$$(Q_1 + Q_2 + Q_3)^2 = 2\,(Q_1^2 + Q_2^2 + Q_3^2)$$

The difference between the two sides is Archimedes' function, which the engine calls the area remainder AR. It equals 16 times the triangle's area squared (it's Heron's formula written in quadrances):

$$A_R = (Q_1 + Q_2 + Q_3)^2 - 2\,(Q_1^2 + Q_2^2 + Q_3^2) = 16\,A^2$$
  • AR = 0: the points are collinear. The middle one adds no new "bend", so in the paper's compression scheme it's redundant and can be pruned, just as Stage 3 prunes common factors.
  • AR > 0: a real triangle, with area \( A = \sqrt{A_R}/4 \).

Finding the 4th point

V4 completes the triangle into a parallelogram-like shape: start at V1, add the two edge vectors toward V2 and V3, and scale by an amount that grows with the triangle's area. In engine version 10:

$$q = 0.65 + 0.45\,\frac{\sqrt{A_R}}{Q_1 + Q_2 + Q_3 + 1},\qquad V_4 = V_1 + q\,\big[(V_2 - V_1) + (V_3 - V_1)\big]$$

With q = 1 this would be exact parallelogram completion (V4 = V2 + V3 − V1). A flat, collinear triple gives q = 0.65; a fat triangle pushes V4 further out.

CHAPTER 10

Tension, torsion and the π/6 lock

The two outer tiers and the three inner tiers can be twisted against each other. When their twist differs by a multiple of 30°, the paper says the system is "locked"; otherwise it's under "shear".

The two fields

Tension (outer: T4, T3)

Slider 0–360°, default 180°. Turns T4 and T3 about the vertical axis by (tension − 180°). T2 also tilts by a quarter of that.

Torsion (inner: T2, T1, T0)

Slider 0–360°, default 150°. Twists T2, T1 and T0 about their own axis by (torsion − 150°); T1 and T0 also tilt by about a third of that, in the opposite direction.

The twist is applied to everything that belongs to a tier: the torus itself, its helix tubes, its particles, the calculation path and every TQF vertex. Move a slider and you'll see the quadrances and AR on the TQF Area Remainder card change immediately (its sub-line shows Q̂ and the current Δθ).

The lock rule

$$\Delta\theta = |\,\text{tension} - \text{torsion}\,|, \qquad \text{locked} \iff \Delta\theta \bmod 30^\circ = 0$$

The defaults give Δθ = 30°, so the engine starts locked (green π/6 LOCKED badge). Any other difference shows an amber SHEAR (x°) badge, where x is the remainder. Because the sliders move in 5° steps, the possible shear remainders are 5°, 10°, 15°, 20° and 25°.

  • Snap π/6 (30°) Lock rounds both sliders to the nearest multiple of 30°, which always produces a lock.
  • Test 15° Shear Twist sets torsion to tension + 45°. 45 mod 30 = 15, the worst possible shear (exactly half a tooth). If Archimedean Triangulation is on, you can watch the polygons twist.

Both pairs of sliders (TELEMETRY and VISIBILITY tabs) control the same two values and stay in sync.

In plain words

Picture two gears, one for the outer rings and one for the inner rings, each with teeth every 30°. When the teeth mesh, motion passes cleanly from one to the other ("zero drag"). Turn one gear by half a tooth and they grind. The paper's 12-6-7-3-1 engine and its "Planck cube" lock are the theoretical version of this picture; the engine itself marks this module [HEURISTIC], meaning a guiding model rather than a proven result.

The differential clutch (CLUTCH tab)

The CLUTCH tab models two clocks running at different rates, and a clutch that engages them in five steps. Everything here feeds back into the 3D tiers.

The 12-10-8 torsional pump

The left drawing shows three nested gears:

  • an outer 12-tooth gear (blue), standing for the tension tiers T4 and T3;
  • a middle 10-tooth clutch gear (purple) that turns the opposite way (σ = −1) and, when the clutch slips, more slowly;
  • an inner 8-tooth anchor gear (amber, red when there is drift), standing for the torsion tiers T1 and T0.

The right drawing is the 8-ray π-cycle rotor: eight spokes at 45° (π/4) intervals. The highlighted spoke is the current step k = 1…8. Spokes 2 and 6 are drawn in amber: these are the two handshake windows, where the rotor points along +i and −i (in the complex plane, (1+i)2 = 2i and (1+i)6 = −8i).

  • +1 Pulse advances everything by one tick: the pulse counter (out of 60), the outer frame (by 12 of 720, i.e. 6°), the rotor step, and the calculation path by one node.
  • Inject Drift introduces a holonomy drift of ||log H|| = 0.342. It twists T1 and T0 by an extra 17° (0.342 × 50°; T2 by half that), turns the inner gear and the scalar core red, and logs an alert.

Two engines, two rates

Choose a local rate for Engine A (60, 40 or 72 Hz) and a remote rate for Engine B (45, 72, 90 or 144 Hz). The engine then computes:

  • Ratio γAB = fB/fA in lowest terms (60 and 90 give 3/2).
  • Boost cosh = (fA² + fB²) / (2 fA fB). For 60/90 this is 13/12 ≈ 1.083.
  • Qhclk = (fA² − fB²)² / (2 fA fB)². For 60/90 this is 25/144 ≈ 0.1736.
Why "cosh"?

These two numbers have a neat meaning. Write the rate ratio as γ = fB/fA. Then the boost is \( \tfrac12(\gamma + 1/\gamma) = \cosh(\ln\gamma) \), the hyperbolic cosine of the rapidity ln γ (the same quantity that adds up velocities in special relativity), and \( Q_h = \cosh^2 - 1 = \sinh^2(\ln\gamma) \), the hyperbolic "spread" between the two clocks. Equal rates give cosh = 1 and Qh = 0: no mismatch to absorb.

The clutch ramp and slip

The Clutch Ramp slider r runs from 0 (barely engaged) to 4 (fully locked). From it the engine derives a slip coefficient λr, an engagement η and an effective frequency:

$$\lambda_r = 2.4\cdot\frac{4 - r}{4},\qquad \eta = \begin{cases} 1 & r = 4 \\[2pt] \dfrac{1}{1 + \lambda_r\,(Q_h + 0.08)} & r < 4\end{cases},\qquad f_{\text{eff}} = (1 - \eta)\,f_A + \eta\,f_B$$

η = 1 means the clutch is locked and the system runs at Engine B's rate. Lower η means slip: the effective rate is a blend of the two. Slip also twists the inner tiers in 3D by (1 − η) × 28° (T2 by half), and turns the core red below η = 0.99.

The 64-bit engram (ENGRAM tab)

An engram is a memory trace. Here it's a 64-bit word laid out as an 8×8 grid, the "Einstein tile", made from a result or any text you type.

Making a tile

  1. Type anything into Engram Grounding Seed (a number, an expression, a word), or press Hash to Engram in the TERMINAL to send the latest result there.
  2. Pick a Form (see below) and press Synthesize 64-Bit Tile.
  3. The 8×8 kernel lights up, the full value appears in hex (e.g. 0x…), and the four region readouts update.
What the tile is (and isn't)

The tile is a fingerprint of the text: two 32-bit hashes in the FNV-1a family are computed from the characters and placed in the top and bottom halves. The same text always gives the same tile, and changing one character changes it completely. It doesn't store the number's value, so 0.3 and 0.30, which are equal numbers, give different tiles. After hashing, the chosen Form ID is written into its own field (bits 43–40).

Reading the grid

Bit 63 is at the top left; bits count down left to right, row by row, to bit 0 at the bottom right. Hover over a cell to see what it means in the bit inspector; click it to flip it. The colours show four regions:

CHAPTER 16

The wider architecture: Loom & Weave and the Zero-Point engine

The calculator is one piece of a larger design. Two companion programs describe what happens before data reaches the five tiers, and what happens at the very centre. This chapter summarises how they fit together. They are separate simulations, not controls in this engine.

Three scales, one pipeline

Macro: Loom & Weave

Funnels an outside, many-dimensional field of data inward and wraps it onto a toroidal memory over a 720-frame cycle.

Meso: this calculator

Carries one calculation through five nested tiers and latches an exact result.

Micro: Zero-Point engine

Looks inside the centre point itself: an 8-step process in which a single point unfolds into a 64-bit crystal cube and a 72-node toroid.

The Loom & Weave in five subsystems

  1. The 64-bit seed with 1/16 symmetry. An 8×8 bit lattice whose rows have hardware roles (structural form, parity, frequency, a 32-bit quaternion core, spin class, amplitude). Four seed bits in one corner are mirrored across both axes to set all 64, so 4/64 = 1/16 of the tile determines the rest.
  2. The outer Hilbert envelope. Three perpendicular planes, each with three concentric 12-point star polygons. Eight times per 720-frame cycle (every 90 frames) the envelope shrinks by a factor of φ−4 (φ = the golden ratio), pumping energy inward.
  3. Six directional faces, two lenses each. Along ±X, ±Y and ±Z, an outer 3×3 "trefoil triad" (three layers riding a trefoil-knot path) feeds a middle 4×4 pandiagonal magic square that acts as a focusing lens.
  4. 54 Fibonacci streams. 6 faces × 9 columns of streams run from the triads through the lenses to the core, wrapped in spiral waves whose amplitude shrinks by powers of φ as they approach the centre. Where a stream passes close to one of 64 orbiting core voxels, a spark marks a collision and it's logged.
  5. The Markov pump funnel and the toroidal weave. At the centre, a nested gear train implements the 12-6-7-3-1 compression protocol: a 12-point ring feeds 6-pointed hexagrams, then 7-pointed heptagrams, then a central scalar ring, with 12 Clifford–Strassen tensor stations around it. The whole core travels round a torus of radius 25 while twisting, and the paths of its 24 outer points braid the "toroidal manifold weave".

Breathing: the 720-frame cycle

Most sizes in the Loom follow sin(πt) as t runs from 0 to 1 over 720 frames. From frame 0 to 360 the field expands (inhale, decompression); from 360 to 720 it collapses back into the centre (exhale, compression). At frame 720, sin π = 0 and everything has converged on the central point. That moment is where the Zero-Point process belongs.

Joining them: the integration roadmap

  • A two-epoch state machine. Rather than jumping from frame 720 straight back to 0, the combined system alternates between Loom & Weave (frames 0–720, collecting collisions into a 64-bit change mask) and Zero-Point (8 steps × 90 sub-frames = 720 micro-frames at the centre, with the Loom frozen at 20% opacity and the camera moving into the core). The Zero-Point result becomes the next seed, the weave is recomputed, and a counter-propagating "phase-conjugate" wave travels back out as the next cycle begins.
  • Matching the scales. The Zero-Point tiers (radii 1.2, 2.8, 5.2, 8.4, 12.4) fit inside the Loom's core rings (2.5, 5, 10, 16) when scaled by about 1.25–1.29: the outer tier lands on the 12 Clifford–Strassen stations at radius 16.
  • 72 kissing nodes. The Zero-Point's third manifold has 12 wraps × 6 channels = 72 nodes; the Loom's 12 stations each carry 3 rings = 6 half-arcs, also 72. The two counts line up one to one.
  • Two ways to run time. Mode A, sequential: run the Loom, pause at frame 720, run the 8 Zero-Point steps, resume; best for watching each system clearly. Mode B, parallel: each of the Loom's eight 90-frame beats runs one Zero-Point step inside the moving core; best for seeing the scales couple in real time.

How this calculator connects

  • The 720-frame holonomic cycle is the same count the calculator's SYNC: HOLONOMIC mode uses.
  • The 12-6-7-3-1 engine is the coupling behind PAPER module 3 and the π/6 lock.
  • Both use a 64-bit, 8×8 tile, though each assigns the bits differently.
  • The 12-10-8 gears and the 8-step π-cycle rotor in the CLUTCH tab echo the eight beats of the Loom and the eight Zero-Point steps.

Troubleshooting & FAQ

The engine shows a black screen or no rings.

The 3D view needs WebGL and loads three.js, Tailwind and the icon font from public CDNs. Check that hardware acceleration is on in your browser, and that an ad-blocker or company network isn't blocking cdnjs.cloudflare.com, cdn.jsdelivr.net or cdn.tailwindcss.com. Then hard-refresh (Ctrl/Cmd + Shift + R).

I pressed = and nothing seems to happen.

Check the display: if it's empty, there's nothing to compute. Make sure the speed isn't at its minimum (0.01× is very slow) and that the play button isn't paused. The right panel switches to REGISTER when a calculation starts.

Why do I get ERR: SINGULARITY?

Division by zero, a minus sign at the start of a number (-3, 2^-2), or unbalanced brackets. Write 0-3 instead of -3. See chapter 8.

Why does the answer only appear at the end?

It's computed immediately but held back until the trace reaches the core, so the animation and the result stay in step. See chapter 7.

The same expression traced a different path this time.

Tick Reset Before Calc in the TERMINAL. Without it, each calculation starts wherever the clock happens to be. The tension, torsion, clutch and drift settings also twist the path, so set them the same too (or use Form 3 Sync and Snap π/6 to return to a clean lock).

A calculation is taking much longer than usual.

The speed is probably high enough that the clock skips node 30 now and then; see the speed tip in chapter 15.

The panels cover the view, especially on a phone.

Use the chevron tabs on the panels' inner edges to slide them away, and the arrow on the site bar to tuck it into its ••• button.

Do I need to sign in?
