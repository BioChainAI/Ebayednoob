Three Compute Tracks

One design runs in three places. The browser runs every exact stage and re-checks the other two. A CPU Python track holds the reference, the static-embedding trainer and small models. A GPU Python track, on your hardware, trains the BERT-sized models. Seven rules keep their numbers comparable, and every result comes back as a receipt that can be checked here.

01

Where this sits

The focus is the Python reference, the first thing the CPU track builds (Phase 1). It is the authority for every exact stage: the browser core must match it line for line, and a CPU twin re-evaluates what the GPU track sends back.

Phase 0 is analysis, measurement spikes and a preregistration. No model code exists yet, on any track. This page says what each track will build, in which phase, and on what machine. Its sources are the read-only survey of four repositories and the literature (with its adversarial cross-check, whose corrections win), the design spec, the two Phase 0 pins (WordNet 3.0 and UD English EWT) and the existing systems’ own pages. Run times are planning figures, marked as estimates. The spikes S1–S7 were still running when it was written, and nothing here quotes them (see Explainer 8).

How to read the marks

here runs in this container: 4 CPU cores, 15 GB RAM, no GPU, with PyPI and GitHub raw reachable. net needs a host that was blocked here in Phase 0; since the network was widened at the start of Phase 1, huggingface.co, the osf.io API, zenodo.org, arxiv.org and aclanthology.org are reachable, while osf.io’s file downloads, cdn.jsdelivr.net, download.pytorch.org, mattmahoney.net and the SimLex host are still blocked. gpu needs a GPU, on your hardware or in the cloud. exact means integers, rationals or bytes, identical in every engine. float means floating point, rounded at every step.

Numbers from the literature carry the survey’s grade. verified: read from the paper’s own page or its official repository. likely: from a search snippet of the primary source, which the survey could not open because arxiv.org and aclanthology.org were blocked here then. secondary: from someone else’s summary. estimate marks a planning cost, not a measurement.

Track
One place where code runs: the browser (JavaScript), the CPU (Python on this machine) or the GPU (Python on a machine with a graphics processor).
Authority
The implementation whose output decides a claim. For every exact stage it is the Python reference; the others must reproduce it. One exception: for compatibility with Owl Academy and BioChain-AI, their own JavaScript is the authority (claim L8).
Trace line, parity
A trace line is one recorded value, such as a token id, a rank list or a hash. Two implementations are at parity when every trace line matches byte for byte.
float64
64-bit floating point. Each operation rounds, so two engines, or two orders of the same sum, can disagree in the last bits.
Hyperboloid
The sheet x0² − x1² − … − xd² = 1 with x0 > 0, which holds hyperbolic space in numbers (the Lorentz model). The Lorentz inner product of two points fixes their distance, so ranking by it ranks by distance.
MAP
Mean average precision. For each query node, rank every other node by distance and score how high its true neighbours come. MAP is the mean over queries; 1.0 means the true neighbours always rank first.
FLOP
One floating-point operation. Training cost is estimated as about 6·N·T FLOPs for N non-embedding parameters and T training tokens: about 2N per token for the forward pass and twice that for the backward pass (Kaplan et al. 2020, arXiv:2001.08361v1, §2.1) verified. The estimate leaves out the embedding parameters, the output layer’s compute and the attention cost that grows with context length.
02

Why three tracks

Two reasons. To compare what exactness buys against learned float models, at matched budgets. And to keep every result re-checkable in a browser, whoever ran it and wherever.

CPU Python · referenceThe authority for every exact stage. Float64 static embeddings. MLMs of 1–10M parameters in Phase 6.
GPU Python · scaleBERT-mini and BERT-small, BabyLM strict, GLUE, HiT, an exploratory HyperCore arm. Phase 7, on your hardware.
Back in the browserEvery result returns as an LBENCH/1 receipt. Snapped tables and heads are re-ranked exactly.

What exactness buys, and what it costs

This system bets on representation and certification, not on optimisation. Exact arithmetic is meant to give addresses that decode back without error, rank lists identical in Python, Node and Chromium, and metrics equal as exact fractions; claims L3 and L4 test this. Floats cannot promise it. Engram Mind’s Eye’s spike B found that float placement in H³ decodes cleanly to depth 12 (50/50) and fails from depth 14 (36/50 at 14, 0/50 at 18). A planning check found that float64 ranking inverts straight paths at depth 11–12; spike S1 measures this properly.

Exactness has its own price. The survey measured exact H³ points at depth 28 with numerators of about 138 bits and denominators of about 79. A planning check found the exact H² tree costs 1.5–2.4 times as many bits. Exactness moves Sala et al.’s precision problem into BigInt arithmetic. It does not remove it (Explainer 1).

Learned float models are the other side. A Lorentz table trained in float64, or a BERT that takes in the addresses, may beat the exact tree or lose to it. The only way to know is to measure both with the same tokenizer, data, seeds and metric code, at matched parameters, dimension, bits or evaluations. No single machine here can run all of that; the seven rules let three machines act as one experiment.

The honest prior

Hyperbolic geometry clearly helps on explicit hierarchies, such as WordNet at low dimension. On general NLP it helps modestly or not at all, and some of the reported gains are unchecked. HELM’s authors report gains of “up to 4%” for fully hyperbolic models, a figure the paper does not define: it matches only their largest single-benchmark gain, about 4 points on OpenbookQA at about 120M parameters, and at about a billion parameters the gains are +2.3 points on MMLU, +1.0 on ARC-Challenging and +1.0 on average, all near chance accuracy (Table 1 of arXiv:2505.24722v2) verified. The Hyperbolic Pre-Trained Language Model claims gains on GLUE, SuperGLUE and extractive QA tasks, but the survey could not see its numbers likely.

Hyperbolic Oracles’ own claim C1, “hyperbolic beats flat”, was refuted: 0 supported, 12 refuted and 1 inconclusive of 13 cells. On B4, hyper3 scored 0.325 against flat3’s 0.334. So every hyperbolic arm on every track has a matched Euclidean control, and the encoder claims planned for Phases 6 and 7 are expected to come out small or null.

Re-checkable by anyone

The browser cannot train a model. It can do something that matters more for trust: recompute. Every exact stage runs in plain JavaScript, on the cores of Hyperbolic Oracles and the orbital core, loaded unchanged. Engram Mind’s Eye showed the method: its browser core matches its Python reference on 849 of 849 parity lines, and the check runs in the page. The Lexicon goes one step further: tables and heads trained on the CPU or GPU are snapped to exact numbers and re-evaluated exactly in the browser. To trust a score, a reader need not trust the machine that trained the table.

The browser track

JavaScript only, with no CDN. Here it runs in Node 22.23.4 and in headless Chromium 153 (Chrome for Testing, driven by Playwright 1.63 from /opt/pw-browsers/chromium); elsewhere, in any browser. Its core, lexicon_core.js, was built in Phase 2: it reproduces every line of the Phase 1 traces, and with it L1, L3(f) and L4(a) were decided, all supported; it reproduced the reference’s L4(b) counts, which the reference decides (Explainer 10). What the browser does not do

It never trains embeddings or encoders. transformers.js runs BERT models in a browser through ONNX Runtime, but it does not train verified. A BERT-Tiny ONNX latency baseline comes later, as a vendored, hash-pinned copy, because cdn.jsdelivr.net is blocked here (huggingface.co was too, until the network was widened in Phase 1).

Why PolyStepH stays small

PolyStepH is Hyperbolic Oracles’ gradient-free optimizer on products of hyperbolic and flat factors (Explainer 1). It needs no gradients, so it runs in plain JavaScript; the survey measured about 2,900 evaluations per second in Node. Its largest model so far had 48 parameters (B4, 2×105 evaluations), and there it did no better than flat. Gradient-free methods in the literature, such as MeZO verified, fine-tune pretrained models; none pretrains from scratch. So the browser tunes heads of tens to a few hundred parameters. A candidate claim for Phase 6, L15, would compare PolyStep with MeZO-style zeroth-order SGD on a fixed head at equal evaluations.

Why the Unicode tables are data

Node 22.23.4 ships Unicode 17.0 (ICU 78.3); Python 3.13.18’s unicodedata is 15.1.0. Code points assigned after 15.1 are known to Node and not to Python, so the two can disagree on their category, case or decomposition. The normaliser therefore reads tables exported from unicodedata 15.1.0 and pinned by SHA-256, and its JavaScript never calls String.prototype.normalize, toLowerCase or \p{} classes. The platform’s own functions are kept as a control that must fail L1. Spike S6 lists where the three runtimes disagree; this page does not quote it.

The CPU Python track

Python 3.13.18 on this machine: 4 cores, 15 GB RAM, 22 GB of free disk. It is the authority for every exact stage, and the place where float64 learning happens until a GPU is in play.

What it builds

  • The reference (Phase 1 on): normaliser, WordPiece and the V0 trainer, the WordNet reader, addresses and id codec, the exact H² tree, exact ranking, the flat arms, the LPS overlay; in Phase 3 the shard grower, manifests, records, attestation and the hardened verifier (chain.py, records.py, manifest.py, attest.py, verify.py). Phase 1 decided L2, L3 (Python side), L5 and L7, all four supported (Explainer 9). In Phase 2 it was the authority the browser core was compared with for L1, L3(f) and L4(a), and it decided L4(b) itself, all supported (Explainer 10). In Phase 3 it was the authority for L8(d), L9 and L10’s verdicts, which the browser core equalled, while Owl Academy’s own code was the authority for L8(a, b); L8–L11 are all supported (Explainer 11). See reference/README.md for the modules and the authority rule.
  • The static-embedding trainer (Phase 4): numpy float64, in the Lorentz model (Nickel & Kiela’s Riemannian SGD) and in flat space (plain SGD), at d ∈ {2, 3, 5, 10, 50}. A Riemannian step projects the gradient onto the hyperboloid’s tangent plane and walks back onto the sheet with the exponential map. Built in Phase 4 (embed.py, its random stream a numba copy of the shared generator checked against hpstep.prng; snapping and exact ranking in learned.py), it decided L6 and L4(c), both supported, L6(c) against its registered prediction (baseline B1; Explainer 12).
  • Tokenizer metrics: fertility, bytes per token, Rényi efficiency and the [UNK] rate, used as gates, not goals (Stage 0).
  • Brown clusters, later.
  • Small MLMs of 1–10M parameters (Phase 6, after the Stage-1 gate). A masked language model (MLM) is trained the way BERT is: hide some tokens and predict them.

06

Seven rules for comparable results

A number from the browser, one from the CPU and one from a GPU can stand side by side only if they share everything except the thing being compared. These seven rules fix the rest.

  1. One tokenizer. A single LVOCAB/1 record names it: the vocabulary’s SHA-256, the hash of the N_B specification, the hash of the Unicode 15.1 tables, the algorithm id and the special tokens. Every track loads the same record. L1 tests that Python, Node and Chromium agree byte for byte on the normalised text, the token strings, the ids, the byte offsets and the SHA-256 of the id stream.
  2. One data identity. Every corpus and split is named by its LCORPUS/1 root. That root is a Merkle root: one 32-byte hash at the top of a tree of hashes over every shard record (RFC 6962). Change any shard and the root changes, and a short proof shows that a given shard is included. Splits and MLM masks are drawn from one shared generator, Rng: xoshiro128** seeded by splitmix32, the same generator in hyperbolic_core.js and in Python’s hpstep/prng.py. So every track masks identically, and anyone can replay the draws. Gated evaluation sets appear by hash only.
  3. One seed scheme. Every random draw takes its seed from one table (below).
  4. One metric implementation. Python is the authority. For exact metrics, the JavaScript reproduces its traces line for line. Float metrics carry registered tolerances and a hash of every item’s output.
  5. Matched budgets. Gradient-free runs are matched on evaluations. Gradient runs record parameters, tokens seen, FLOPs (reported the ELUE way verified), hardware, library versions, thread count and wall-clock time. Exact representations are compared at matched dimension, and also at matched bits, in the sense of Sala et al.’s precision–dimension trade-off: their combinatorial embedding reaches WordNet MAP 0.989 in 2 dimensions, but needs about 500 bits verified (from the abstract, confirmed by the cross-check).
  6. Pinned scorers. Masked LMs are scored on BLiMP by PLL-word-l2r (Kauf & Ivanova 2023 likely) at a pinned babylm/evaluation-pipeline-2025 commit. A pseudo-log-likelihood (PLL) masks each token in turn and adds up the log-probabilities the model gives the hidden tokens. Plain PLL inflates the scores of words split into several pieces; the word-l2r variant also masks the rest of the word to the right. The GLUE fine-tuning recipe is fixed before any decisive run. The pin is needed because the numbers disagree: the 2024 pipeline gives LTG-BERT 60.6 on BLiMP strict-small verified, while figures near 80 for LTG-BERT and ELC-BERT (Table 1 of arXiv:2410.24159v2) verified come from 2023-era papers under a different pipeline. The 2024 README itself flags its strict-small LTG-BERT score as lower than expected, possibly from too few training epochs.
  7. Cross-track anchors. The browser exactly re-evaluates every table and head trained on the CPU or GPU. A fixed BLiMP subsample, 67 × 100 pairs drawn with Rng(1000+i), is scored on both CPU and GPU within a registered tolerance. A CPU twin re-evaluates each GPU checkpoint. Receipts are classed reproducible or attested (chapter 8).

L2 sits in Stage 0 for a reason. engram/1, the key inherited from Engram Mind’s Eye, keeps only the parity of each letter’s count (GF(2) rank 26). twinShaThe output hash of the twin run

A twin, here, is a second run of the same evaluation in another engine or on another machine: the browser beside the Python reference, or the CPU beside a GPU. (It is not Hyperbolic Oracles’ twin system, two tri-channels in H³ on one clock.) Records are SHA-256 hashed and can be signed in BioChain-AI’s ATTEST/1 format, with ECDSA P-256 (Explainer 4).

reproducible

The twin’s output is bit-identical. Exact stages are built to land here: the browser recomputes them from the same inputs and must match the reference line for line.

attested

A float result from a single run. The receipt says who ran it, on what machine and with which libraries, and holds every item’s output hash. It does not prove the number is right, and it is labelled so.

How a GPU result comes home

  1. The run, on your GPU, writes an LBENCH/1 receipt and an LMODEL/1 record. LMODEL/1 is a per-file hash manifest of the model in the style of sigstore’s model-transparency, with the training seed, the LCORPUS root, the mask-schedule seed and checkpoint hashes by training step (a light form of proof of learning).
  2. The records are verified here: the hashes are recomputed from the files they name.
  3. A CPU twin re-evaluates the checkpoint. It scores the fixed BLiMP subsample within the registered tolerance.
  4. The browser re-ranks exactly any table or head that was snapped to exact numbers.
  5. The receipt is classed: reproducible if the twin’s output is bit-identical, attested if it is not.

The contamination receipt

A score means little if the test sentences were in the training data. So each corpus carries a contamination receipt: the SHA-256 of every 13-gram (run of 13 tokens) in the evaluation items, intersected with the training id streams. Only the counts are published, never the text.

What a receipt does not prove

A signature proves who committed a record. It does not prove that the record’s licence holds or that its data is correct. An attested number is one float run’s output. A record with no valid attestation is never called verified, and language shards keep the same split: their verdicts are reported separately as self-consistent and authentic.
